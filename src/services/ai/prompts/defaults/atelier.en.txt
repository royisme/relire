The join rule, which the app checks character for character:
- Bricks are joined with one space.
- No space after a brick that ends in an apostrophe: J’ + aime gives J’aime.
- No space before a punctuation brick: plage + . gives plage.
- Apostrophes are the typographic ’, never '.
- No no-break space: write Où vas-tu? with no space before ?.
- The first brick carries the capital letter; the last brick is the closing punctuation brick.
- A fused form (au, aux, du, des) is one brick, never two.
- sentence must equal the join of bricks exactly.

Brick roles (use the ID exactly as written):
- subject: the doer (je, nous, Marie)
- verb: the conjugated main verb
- auxiliary: avoir or être in a compound tense
- noun: a noun (plage, loyer)
- determiner: an article or determiner (le, un, ma, ce)
- preposition: à, de, en, pour, dans
- contraction: a fused form (au, aux, du, des)
- adjective: an adjective, agreed with its noun
- adverb: souvent, très, demain
- negation: ne, pas, plus, jamais
- conjunction: et, mais, parce que, si
- pronoun: a pronoun that is not the subject (le, lui, y, en)
- expression: a fixed chunk of several words (il y a, est-ce que)
- punctuation: the closing mark (., ?, !)

Rules:
- bricks are listed in the order of sentence; each brick is one word or one fixed expression.
- distractors: 2 to 4 per item. Each is wrong for a real learner reason that belongs to the item's grammar point (a wrong-gender article, à le instead of au, a missing or extra preposition, a wrong person or form), and its one-line reason, in both languages, teaches that rule. A distractor must never spell the same as a brick of the sentence and must never complete another correct sentence.
- variants: only other correct orders built from exactly the same bricks (for example a fronted adverbial); otherwise [].
- links: all the grammatical connections of the sentence, each one between the item's own bricks. head is the index, starting at 0, of the governing brick; dependents are the indexes of the bricks that hang off it; label is the real grammar term shown on the connection (COD, à + nom, accord f. sg., sujet); note is one line, in both languages, stating the rule. List every connection: the subject to its verb (an imperative has none), what the verb takes, each article to its noun, each agreement, each negation pair, each pronoun and each modifier.
- the relation of a link is one of these (use the ID exactly as written):
  - subject: the subject of a verb
  - object: a direct object (COD)
  - complement: what a verb takes through a preposition or an infinitive (à + nom, de + infinitif)
  - article: a determiner to its noun
  - agreement: gender or number agreement, for example adjective to noun
  - negation: the two parts of ne … pas around a verb
  - modifier: an adverb or other modifier to its head
  - pronoun: an object pronoun to its verb
- modify: only the last item has it. One change — negative, plural, subject or question — with a bilingual instruction, the new sentence, its complete bricks and its own links over them. The new sentence must differ from the original.

Check these grammar rules before answering; models often get them wrong:
- After a negation, un/une/des/du/de la become de (d’ before a vowel) only on a direct object and after il y a — never after être, never after a preposition: Je ne vais pas à une fête keeps une.
- aimer, adorer and détester keep the definite article in the negative.
- Cities take à with no article (à Montréal); countries take au, en or aux (au Canada, en France).
- à + le = au, à + les = aux, de + le = du, de + les = des; à l’ and de l’ never fuse.
- regarder, écouter, attendre and chercher take a direct object with no preposition.
- A profession after être takes no article: Il est médecin.
- Register follows the situation: tutoiement with a friend, vouvoiement with an examiner or an institution.
